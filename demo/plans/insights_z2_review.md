## 3. The potentially important new direction: composite matter

This is where I would concentrate the theoretical work.

Aziz and Howl's calculation uses a simplified scalar-field description of the matter constituents. Their expression contains the constituent mass \\(m\\), total mass \\(M\\), and radius \\(R\\).

The leading quantum-gravity phase, by comparison, depends on the total mass distribution.

That suggests a potentially useful discriminator:

Do two objects with matched macroscopic mass and geometry exhibit different gravitationally mediated quantum correlations because their underlying matter constituents differ?

Consider two systems with approximately equal \\(M\\) and \\(R\\), but different compositions.

The leading quantum-gravity prediction should depend on their mass distributions, not on an arbitrary choice of constituent-field description.

The Aziz–Howl correction, in its simplified model, has additional constituent-mass dependence.

That creates a candidate material-composition test.

But there is a major mathematical obligation before turning this into an experimental prediction: replace the simplified scalar constituents with a realistic composite-matter description, including the relevant nuclear, electronic, binding-energy and collective degrees of freedom.

We cannot simply substitute atomic masses into their formula and declare the result a prediction for real nanodiamonds.

This is the part I would investigate for a potentially publishable contribution. The novelty is not yet established; a proper prior-art review is required.

## 4. What would count as a discriminator?

I would combine three measurements rather than rely on a single entanglement result.

First: time dependence. Reconstruct the output-state evolution at multiple interaction durations. The leading quantum-gravity phase and the Aziz–Howl higher-order contribution have different perturbative time dependence. For the latter, \\(\vartheta\\) scales as \\(t^2\\) in their calculation, whereas the leading phase scales as \\(t\\). The actual measured entanglement witness must be calculated from the complete output state, not assumed to inherit either power law directly.&#x20;

Second: mass and composition dependence. Test whether the predicted response follows total mass and geometry alone or also carries the constituent dependence of a particular classical-metric/QFT-matter model.

Third: long-time behavior. Test whether the observed evolution agrees with unitary perturbative predictions, a particular DP-type collapse model, or a stochastic/hybrid model.

A positive entanglement result could rule out some models while remaining compatible with others. A measured response outside the predictions of several pre-registered classical alternatives would be much stronger evidence for the specified quantum-gravity model.

It would still not prove that every conceivable classical theory is impossible.

### Item 5 — The quantum/classical border: type III is the real environment

The real environment in algebraic QFT is type III₁ (Reeh–Schlieder). Type III algebras have no trace, no minimal projections, no pure states. **The actualization problem might look different — and perhaps be resolved — if the environment is treated as type III.**

**The falsifiable discriminator:** If the environment is type III, the decoherence rate should be set by the modular flow, not by the system-environment coupling strength. This is testable in principle: compare decoherence rates across different physical systems coupled to type III environments (e.g., a qubit coupled to a quantum field vs. a qubit coupled to a finite-dimensional bath). The prediction is that the type III case has a rate set by the modular structure, not by the coupling.

### Item 7 — Far-from-equilibrium: thermalization is generic, so the question is failure

The document's framing: "Equilibrium stat mech is a crown jewel; non-equilibrium is patchwork. A 'second law for structure' is missing."

**The net new insight from the camera audit:** The Lévy lemma (measure concentration) says thermalization is *generic* — a random pure state on a high-dimensional Hilbert space has a reduced state exponentially close to the maximally mixed state. **Thermalization is not a dynamical process requiring ergodicity; it is a measure-theoretic fact requiring only high dimension.** The interesting question is not "why does thermalization happen?" but "why does it *fail*?"

**The falsifiable discriminator:** The Lévy lemma's hypotheses are: high dimension, rotation-invariant measure, 1-Lipschitz observables. Thermalization should fail if and only if one of these hypotheses fails. The failures are: low dimension (few-body systems), non-generic states (integrable systems, MBL), and non-rotation-invariant measures (systems with a preferred state). The discriminator: **a system should fail to thermalize if and only if its state is not in the equivalence class of rotation-invariant measures.** This is a sharp criterion. It predicts that MBL and integrability are not separate phenomena — they are the same phenomenon (non-generic measure) viewed from different angles. This is testable in cold-atom experiments: prepare a system in a non-generic state and measure whether it thermalizes; the prediction is that it won't, and the failure rate should follow the Lévy bound.

**Why the field underweights it:** The far-from-equilibrium community (turbulence, active matter, glassy dynamics) and the quantum thermalization community (ETH, MBL) are separate fields with separate conferences. The Lévy lemma is known to both, but the connection — that thermalization is generic and failure is the interesting case — is not the organizing principle of either field.
