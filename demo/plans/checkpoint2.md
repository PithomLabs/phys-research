## prompt

## answer 

Yes. Your understanding is correct. The Three Mathematical Reality Checks must be applied continuously, starting at the planning stage—not introduced only after the equations have been collected.

In fact, I would strengthen the research protocol's operational interpretation: the three checks are not a final review checklist. They are a standing discipline governing every research decision, from choosing a question to evaluating the final result.

There is an important principle behind this:

A published physics paper is evidence of what its authors have constructed and argued. It is not, by itself, proof that every assumption, mathematical connection or physical interpretation is sound.

We should neither accept a result because it is published nor reject it simply because it relies on assumptions. We must expose those assumptions, understand their consequences and determine exactly what follows from them.

## 1. Apply the three checks at every stage

### RC1 — Mathematical Landscape

At every stage, ask:

- What mathematical structures are being used?
- What does each structure represent physically?
- What assumptions make it applicable?
- What is its domain and regime of validity?
- What does it describe, and what does it leave out?
- Are we using the mathematics for something it was never designed to describe?

At the planning stage, this applies to the proposed research itself. We must question whether our selected mathematical framework actually covers the physical phenomenon we intend to investigate.

### RC2 — Mathematical Intersection

At every stage, ask:

- Which mathematical structures must interact to explain the phenomenon?
- Why must they interact?
- What does their intersection actually produce?
- Are we combining compatible structures or merely placing equations beside one another?
- What happens if one structure, assumption or relationship is removed or altered?
- Are we claiming minimality without demonstrating it?

At the planning stage, this means challenging the proposed R1/R4 connection before committing resources to it.

### RC3 — Mathematical Interoperability

At every stage, ask:

- How does one mathematical framework communicate with another?
- What is the actual mapping?
- What assumptions make that mapping valid?
- What properties are preserved?
- What information is lost or approximated?
- What is the applicable physical regime?
- What must be proved, and what would falsify the connection?

At the planning stage, this means challenging whether the proposed research workflow can genuinely establish interoperability, rather than merely collect compatible-looking results from different papers.

## 2. How this changes JT-ISLAND-002

The current plan places the consolidated Three Reality Checks primarily in Phase F. That is insufficient.

They should operate as recurring checkpoints:

## Continuous research discipline

1. Planning and model selection

   Challenge the research question, candidate assumptions, mathematical coverage and proposed R1/R4 intersections.
2. Primary-source investigation

   Challenge the authors' definitions, axioms, assumptions, approximations, derivations and physical interpretations.
3. Equation reconstruction

   Reconstruct from the stated premises. Check mathematical consistency, applicability and missing dependencies.
4. Cross-framework integration

   Establish explicit bridges. Test what is preserved, lost, assumed and falsifiable.
5. Phys-Pseudo formalization

   Ensure the representation does not silently strengthen, weaken or change the original physics.
6. Implementation and verification

   Check that executable tests establish the mathematical properties they claim to test, within their actual scope.
7. Independent adversarial review

   Reapply all three checks to the complete research artifact and challenge any conclusions that exceed the evidence.

Standing requirement

Every substantive research decision remains subject to RC1, RC2 and RC3.

## 3. One important distinction: first principles versus inherited results

We should investigate from first principles, but that does not mean pretending every established theorem or physical theory must be independently rediscovered.

Instead, distinguish:

- Definitions: What mathematical objects are we actually talking about?
- Assumptions: What are we accepting provisionally?
- Derivations: What genuinely follows from those assumptions?
- Imported results: What are we relying on from another investigation?
- Approximations: What has been neglected, and under what conditions?
- Physical evidence: What establishes that the mathematical description corresponds to reality?

For example, in JT-ISLAND-002, QRT is an imported prescription. We can test whether its application to the RST–GSST construction is mathematically consistent and physically justified within its stated scope. We cannot claim to have derived QRT merely because we reconstructed the Page curve.

That distinction is fundamental to scientific honesty.
