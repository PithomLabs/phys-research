# Understanding Gravity: What We Have Learned So Far
## A plain-English technical account of the search for a common mathematical foundation for matter, spacetime and dynamics

**Research status: November 6, 2026**

### Executive Summary

Physics has achieved remarkable success in describing the universe, but its most successful theories do not describe reality using the same mathematical language.

Quantum mechanics describes matter in terms of states, probabilities and operators.

General relativity describes gravity through the geometry and curvature of spacetime.

Quantum field theory describes particles and their interactions through fields and mathematical operators.

Thermodynamics and statistical mechanics describe large collections of physical systems through probability, statistics and information.

Each framework works remarkably well within its intended territory. The difficulty appears when we try to make them work together.

Our investigation has uncovered a potentially important direction: perhaps the central problem is not simply that we need another theory of gravity. Perhaps we also need a more rigorous mathematical framework for translating between the different descriptions of physical reality.

This is the idea behind our developing **common mathematical language**.

We have also learned that a theory should not be judged by how elegantly it describes one phenomenon. It should be tested against multiple independent problems using the same standards.

We are developing two competing approaches for that exercise:

- **BHC:** Bohmian matter interacting with classical spacetime.
- **ALG:** An observer-based operator-algebra framework in which gravity is quantum in a perturbative regime.

Neither is established as a complete theory of nature. Both are research candidates whose assumptions and limitations must be exposed.

Our investigation currently revolves around five major problems: the quantum-to-classical gravity connection, the nature of time, quantum measurement, black-hole information and the cosmological constant.

The objective is not to make either approach win. It is to discover exactly where its mathematics works, where it fails and what remains unexplained.

# 1. The fundamental problem: physics speaks several mathematical languages

Consider three descriptions of the same universe.

**Matter**

Matter is described through quantum states, physical observables, probability distributions and interactions.

For example, quantum mechanics can describe an electron as a state containing several possible measurement outcomes, with probabilities assigned to them.

**Spacetime**

General relativity describes the universe as a four-dimensional spacetime whose geometry can change in response to matter and energy.

Gravity is not simply another force acting inside space. It is connected to the structure of spacetime itself.

**Dynamics**

Dynamics describes how physical systems change.

In ordinary quantum mechanics, a Hamiltonian determines how a quantum state evolves. In general relativity, the equations also involve constraints associated with the freedom to choose coordinates and time descriptions.

The difficulty is that these descriptions cannot always be translated into one another without losing important information.

Imagine three engineers who each designed a highly sophisticated machine, but one speaks English, another Spanish and the third French.

They may understand their own machines perfectly. However, when they connect them, they discover that some concepts have no direct translation.

That is approximately the situation we face.

The challenge is not simply to translate mathematical symbols. It is to preserve the physical meaning of the quantities being translated.

A valid translation must preserve:

- What is physically observable.
- What remains unchanged under a change of reference frame or description.
- How probabilities are calculated.
- How physical systems evolve.
- How matter influences spacetime.
- How spacetime influences matter.

Our working conclusion is that these connections need explicit mathematical contracts rather than informal assumptions.

# 2. The common mathematical language

We have identified three mathematical foundations that could serve as the starting point.

| Physical domain | Mathematical description | What it represents |
|---|---|---|
| Matter | Operator algebras and quantum states | Physical quantities and their possible outcomes |
| Spacetime | Differential geometry | Geometry, distance, curvature and causality |
| Dynamics | Evolution operators and constraints | How physical systems change |

The important development is that we are no longer treating these as three independent subjects.

We are investigating the mathematical connections between them.

### Connection A: Matter and spacetime

We need to establish how quantum matter is represented on a particular spacetime.

For example, we should be able to describe a quantum field on a curved spacetime and recover ordinary quantum field theory when gravity becomes negligible.

This is an established research direction in locally covariant quantum field theory.

### Connection B: Spacetime and dynamics

We need to establish how spacetime geometry determines the allowed evolution of physical systems.

General relativity already provides a sophisticated framework for this through its Hamiltonian and constraint formulations.

However, the interpretation of time becomes more difficult when the gravitational field itself is quantized.

### Connection C: Matter and dynamics

We need to establish how physical states evolve and how probabilities emerge from that evolution.

Ordinary quantum mechanics supplies a well-developed mathematical structure for this connection.

However, the situation becomes more complicated when the system has no ordinary external clock or when the mathematical evolution operator is not well-defined.

### What would make these connections reliable?

We are introducing three requirements.

**First: preserve meaning.**

Every physical quantity must have a clearly declared mathematical home. Its units, dimensions, assumptions and interpretation must be explicit.

**Second: perform a round-trip test.**

A translation between two mathematical frameworks should reproduce a known result when we return to an established physical situation.

For example:

- A quantum field on a fixed spacetime should reproduce ordinary quantum field theory.
- The Hamiltonian formulation of general relativity should reproduce Einstein's equations.
- The mathematical description of a simple harmonic oscillator should reproduce its familiar quantum evolution.

**Third: test the limits.**

Two important consistency checks are:

- When gravitational effects become negligible, recover ordinary quantum field theory.
- When quantum effects become negligible, recover classical general relativity for the same matter system.

These requirements are not a proof that a universal mathematical foundation exists. They are the tests that a proposed foundation must pass.

There is already substantial mathematical machinery supporting these connections. What remains unknown is whether all three can be combined into one consistent framework beyond the regimes where existing approximations work.

# 3. The intersection principle: solving problems at the boundaries

One of our most useful insights is what we call the **intersection principle**.

Instead of asking which mathematical field independently solves a particular problem, we ask:

**What is the smallest combination of mathematical structures necessary to derive the phenomenon?**

Consider black-hole information.

The problem involves at least three different ingredients:

- Quantum information and the preservation of quantum evolution.
- Spacetime geometry and black-hole horizons.
- Statistical entropy and probability.

Looking at only one of these ingredients cannot provide the complete answer.

The same reasoning applies to other problems.

| Problem | Mathematical structures that must be connected |
|---|---|
| Quantum gravity | Quantum states, geometry and dynamics |
| Cosmological constant | Spacetime curvature, vacuum energy and the space of possible physical states |
| Quantum measurement | Quantum states, probability and actual physical records |
| Arrow of time | Dynamics, statistical mechanics and the selection of initial conditions |
| Black-hole information | Geometry, quantum information and entropy |

This leads to a different research strategy.

Rather than endlessly extending an individual mathematical framework, investigate whether the missing connection between two or more frameworks is where the unresolved physics lives.

There is an important qualification: this is a research heuristic, not a mathematical theorem. It helps us identify promising directions, but it does not guarantee that an intersection will resolve a particular problem.

# 4. Two competing approaches to gravity

We are now comparing two different ways of constructing a physical theory.

They begin with different assumptions about what is fundamentally real.

## Approach A: BHC — Bohmian matter with classical gravity

The central idea is that matter has an actual physical configuration, even when its quantum description contains several possibilities.

In Bohmian mechanics, the wave function guides the actual configuration of matter.

For example, an electron is not simply treated as a collection of possible measurement outcomes. There is an actual configuration, while the wave function determines its dynamics.

Our proposed extension takes this one step further.

What if matter follows Bohmian mechanics, but spacetime remains classical?

In that case:

- Matter has an actual configuration.
- The wave function guides its evolution.
- Gravity remains a classical spacetime geometry.
- The actual matter configuration, rather than simply the average quantum expectation, contributes to the gravitational source.

This last point is especially important.

In standard semiclassical gravity, the source is commonly represented using the expectation value of the quantum stress-energy tensor.

Our proposed BHC framework instead investigates a source constructed from the actual configuration and the wave function.

The proposed gravitational equation is:

Gμν + Λgμν = 8πG Sμν[Q, Ψ]

In plain English, the curvature of spacetime responds to the physical matter configuration described by the Bohmian model.

**The difficult question:** Can this source consistently satisfy the conservation laws required by general relativity?

It must satisfy:

∇μSμν = 0

This is not optional. Einstein's equations and the underlying geometric identities require it.

We have not established that the proposed source satisfies this requirement for general physical configurations.

It might work only for a restricted class of states. That distinction could determine whether this approach is viable.

Other unresolved questions include whether its predictions depend on the choice of clock or preferred foliation, and whether it can account for black-hole information.

## Approach B: ALG — Observer-based operator-algebra gravity

The second approach starts from a different perspective.

Instead of treating individual quantum states as the primary objects, it places greater emphasis on the mathematical structure of physical observables.

An observer does not have direct access to the entire universe. The observer measures quantities available within a particular physical region.

The mathematical language of operator algebras provides a sophisticated way to describe these observables and their relationships.

The interesting development is that researchers have found ways to incorporate the observer's position, gravitational constraints and clock into this mathematical structure.

One particularly important result is the construction of a Type II₁ algebra of observables for a static region of de Sitter spacetime.

This construction provides a natural mathematical treatment of entropy, reproducing the expected generalized gravitational entropy for semiclassical states, up to an additive constant.

It is significant because it shows that operator-algebra methods can connect quantum information, observers, spacetime and gravitational entropy.

However, this is not a complete theory of quantum gravity.

Our ALG research branch investigates whether such methods can be extended into a consistent framework with:

- Observer-dependent physical observables.
- Quantum probability.
- Internal clocks.
- Gravitational constraints.
- A mathematically consistent treatment of entropy.

The construction is perturbative in gravitational coupling in the relevant branch of our proposal.

It also assumes that the necessary quantum field theory algebras already exist.

That means it inherits an important unresolved problem: the rigorous mathematical construction of interacting quantum field theories in four spacetime dimensions.

**The difficult question:** Can this approach explain why an observer obtains one definite physical outcome, rather than merely describing the available observables and their probability distributions?

At present, it has no established answer to that question.

## Why compare them?

The approaches disagree about fundamental physical assumptions.

| Question | BHC | ALG |
|---|---|---|
| Is there an actual matter configuration? | Yes | Not assumed |
| Is gravity classical? | Yes | Quantum perturbatively |
| Is the wave function fundamental? | No, it is not the whole physical reality | Not the starting primitive |
| Is time external? | Retained through a foliation, with additional invariance requirements | Reconstructed through an internal clock |
| How is probability represented? | Equivariance and typicality | Mathematical states on observable algebras |
| How are definite outcomes represented? | Through actual configurations | Not yet established |

We are deliberately keeping both approaches alive.

The objective is to discover which assumptions are necessary, which are unnecessary and which create contradictions.

# 5. Five major tests that every approach must face

We have organized our investigation around five problems.

The important change is that both approaches must face the same problems using the same evaluation standards.

### Test 1: Can quantum matter consistently interact with classical spacetime?

This is the first major challenge.

Einstein's equations tell us how matter and energy influence spacetime.

Quantum theory tells us that matter can exist in superpositions and that its physical properties are described probabilistically.

What happens when we combine these descriptions?

For BHC, the question is whether the gravitational source constructed from actual matter configurations is mathematically consistent.

For ALG, the question is whether the interaction can be recovered from its perturbative gravitational and observer-based structure.

We must check conservation, causal consistency and the validity of the approximation.

### Test 2: What is time?

In ordinary life, time appears to be something that exists independently.

We look at a clock, observe its movement and describe everything else relative to it.

But general relativity and quantum gravity complicate this picture.

In some formulations of quantum gravity, the universe is represented by a global state subject to constraints, rather than evolving against an ordinary external time parameter.

One possible solution is to use an internal clock.

Instead of saying that the universe changes with respect to an external time, we describe one part of the universe changing relative to another part that serves as a clock.

This is the idea behind the Page-Wootters approach.

Our question is whether different clock choices produce mathematically equivalent physical descriptions.

An ideal clock is relatively straightforward. Realistic clocks have limitations, including restricted energy ranges and imperfect time resolution.

The theory must survive these complications.

### Test 3: Why do we observe one outcome?

This is one of the most important conceptual discoveries in our investigation.

We have realized that two questions are often mixed together.

**Question A: Where do the probabilities come from?**

Gleason's theorem shows that, under specific mathematical assumptions, probability assignments on the projection structure of a Hilbert space must take the Born-rule form.

This is an important result.

However, its assumptions matter. It does not establish the Born rule without qualification in every conceivable framework.

**Question B: Why does one particular outcome actually happen?**

Knowing the probability of an outcome does not automatically explain why a particular outcome becomes a physical record.

For example, knowing that a coin has a fifty-percent chance of landing heads does not explain why the coin actually lands heads.

We call this remaining issue the **actualization problem**.

The distinction matters because different interpretations address it differently.

- Bohmian mechanics provides an actual configuration.
- Collapse theories modify the dynamics.
- Everettian approaches interpret the resulting branches differently.

Our BHC branch attempts to address actualization through its physical ontology.

Our ALG branch has a mathematical account of observables and probability, but still needs an account of definite outcomes.

This is a major unresolved difference between them.

### Test 4: Can a theory explain black-hole information?

Black holes appear to create a problem when quantum information is considered.

If something enters a black hole and the black hole eventually evaporates, what happens to the information describing what went inside?

The island formula and replica-wormhole calculations have made substantial progress toward reproducing the expected Page curve of black-hole radiation.

But there are qualifications.

The relevant results are established in particular controlled settings, including certain anti-de Sitter and flat-spacetime-with-bath models.

They cannot automatically be transferred to a universe with de Sitter geometry.

Furthermore, reproducing the Page curve is not the same as providing a complete microscopic explanation of how information is preserved.

Our test is therefore straightforward:

Can BHC and ALG reproduce the relevant information-theoretic results from their own assumptions?

Or must they import mathematical ingredients that contradict their original foundations?

This could be one of the strongest tests of whether classical gravity can survive our proposed comparison.

### Test 5: Why does the cosmological constant have its observed value?

The cosmological constant is connected to the accelerated expansion of the universe.

Quantum field theory also raises a difficult question about the gravitational influence of vacuum energy.

One proposed alternative framework is unimodular gravity.

It changes the mathematical formulation of Einstein's equations so that the cosmological constant appears as an integration constant.

This is interesting because it changes the question from:

Why does the vacuum energy produce this particular cosmological constant?

To:

What determines the integration constant?

But this does not calculate the observed value.

An important correction from our investigation is that classical unimodular gravity is equivalent to general relativity with a cosmological constant as an integration constant.

We should not claim that standard unimodular gravity automatically predicts a different gravitational lensing effect or has already solved the vacuum-energy problem.

The question of whether the observed dark-energy behavior is consistent with a truly constant cosmological term also remains open.

If evolving dark energy were established robustly, a pure constant-Λ explanation would be insufficient.

For now, this remains a conditional test rather than an established falsification.

# 6. A major discovery: gravitational entanglement does not automatically prove quantum gravity

This is one of the most important developments in our recent investigation.

The original gravitationally induced entanglement proposals were exciting because they appeared to offer a laboratory test of the quantum nature of gravity.

The basic idea is simple.

Imagine two small masses.

Each mass is placed into a quantum superposition of two possible locations.

They are allowed to interact gravitationally.

If they become quantum-entangled, the original argument suggested that gravity must have transmitted quantum information.

However, recent theoretical work has complicated that conclusion.

Aziz and Howl published a 2025 paper demonstrating that a particular classical-gravity construction coupled to quantum field theory matter can also generate entanglement through quantum matter interactions.

The Diósi-Penrose model has also been shown to predict gravitationally induced entanglement under its particular assumptions.

In October 2026, Andrea Di Biagio published a further analysis explaining why the assumptions behind the original no-go arguments do not establish a theory-independent result.

The key distinction is between two meanings of locality:

- Locality in ordinary spacetime.
- Locality in the sense of how quantum information can be transmitted.

These are not interchangeable.

Consequently:

**Observing gravitationally induced entanglement would be important, but it would not automatically prove that the gravitational field itself is quantum.**

We need to determine which physical model predicts the observed result.

Our proposed experimental comparison therefore investigates how the signal changes with:

- Total mass.
- Physical size.
- Separation.
- Superposition size.
- Interaction duration.
- Material composition.

We are particularly interested in whether different materials with similar overall mass and geometry produce different predictions because of their underlying constituents.

This could potentially distinguish a model that depends only on the overall mass distribution from one that also depends on the underlying matter-field description.

However, the composite-matter calculation has not yet been completed, and its novelty has not been established.

The research question has therefore changed.

We are no longer simply asking whether gravity can generate entanglement.

We are asking:

**Can we identify which gravitational theory generated the entanglement by comparing its precise mathematical predictions against competing theories?**

# 7. What the mathematical investigation has revealed beyond gravity

The broader investigation has also exposed several important research directions.

### A. Measure theory and the selection problem

Many unresolved questions involve selecting one possibility from a collection of possibilities.

Examples include:

- Why does one measurement outcome occur?
- Why does the universe have its particular initial conditions?
- Why does nature have its observed physical constants?
- Why is this particular vacuum physically realized?

Probability theory and measure theory may be essential to these questions.

This suggests that measure theory deserves a more central role in fundamental physics.

However, we do not yet have a theorem showing that measure theory alone resolves these problems.

### B. Operator algebras and the structure of physical reality

Quantum field theory in curved spacetime suggests that the mathematical structure of observables is more complicated than the finite-dimensional Hilbert-space models commonly used in introductory explanations.

In particular, local quantum field theory often involves Type III operator algebras.

These structures lack certain familiar properties of ordinary quantum systems.

The distinction between Type I, Type II and Type III algebras may be important when investigating entropy, observers, probability and quantum measurement.

One possible research direction is to investigate whether modular theory helps clarify the relationship between quantum environments and physical records.

But the proposed idea of a universal decoherence rate determined by modular flow is still speculative. It is not an established physical prediction.

### C. Thermalization and why it sometimes fails

The Lévy lemma and related measure-concentration results show why certain properties of high-dimensional quantum systems are overwhelmingly typical.

This offers another way to understand why thermal behavior can emerge.

However, it does not establish that every failure of thermalization is explained by a single measure-theoretic condition.

The interesting research direction is to identify precisely which mathematical assumptions fail in integrable systems, many-body localized systems and other non-equilibrium situations.

### D. The actualization problem

The distinction between probability and physical outcomes may be even more fundamental than we initially appreciated.

A mathematical framework can describe possible outcomes and assign probabilities without explaining why a particular outcome becomes an actual record.

This is a genuine gap in our present comparison.

It should be treated as its own research problem, rather than hidden inside the general measurement problem.

# 8. How we are preventing ourselves from fooling ourselves

An important methodological improvement is that we are developing common evaluation rules.

Every proposed theory must be evaluated using the same standards.

We distinguish six possible results:

| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Derives | The result follows from the theory's own assumptions without additional assumptions. |
| Explains | The result follows with a small number of justified additional assumptions. |
| Accommodates | The result can be reproduced by choosing something the theory does not independently determine. |
| Postulates | The result is simply introduced as an assumption. |
| Refutes | A mathematical contradiction or counterexample disproves a particular assumption. |
| Blocked | The derivation cannot be completed because a specific mathematical obstruction remains. |

This distinction is crucial.

A theory should not receive credit for deriving something it merely assumes.

Equally, a theory should not be declared completely false because one particular assumption fails.

The failed assumption must be identified, and the rest of the theory must be examined independently.

We also introduce an assumption graph.

Imagine every assumption as a point on a map.

When several assumptions are needed together to derive a result, we connect those points.

If an assumption appears repeatedly across several different problems, it is probably part of the theory's structural foundation rather than an isolated technical convenience.

This allows us to identify theories that appear powerful only because they repeatedly rely on additional assumptions.

Finally, every mathematical proposal must pass a basic reality check:

1. Are the units and dimensions correct?
2. Does it reproduce the established theory in the appropriate limit?
3. Does the proposed quantity remain meaningful in the limit being used?
4. Is there a simple example that demonstrates the calculation?
5. Is there an alternative model that could produce the same observation?
6. What is the strongest mathematical objection?

A theory must also declare its physical regime.

A result derived for weak gravitational fields and low-energy quantum matter cannot automatically be applied to the interior of a black hole or the earliest moments of the universe.

This is why energy scales, approximation limits and effective-theory assumptions must accompany every conclusion.

# 9. What we have actually established versus what remains hypothetical

It is important to separate mathematical progress from physical discovery.

| Finding | Current assessment |
|---|---|
| General relativity and quantum field theory have mathematically well-defined regimes of application. | Established within their applicable regimes. |
| Gleason's theorem constrains probability assignments under specific assumptions. | Established theorem. |
| Operator-algebra methods can describe observer-dressed gravitational observables and generalized entropy in particular constructions. | Established within those constructions. |
| Classical-gravity models can generate entanglement under particular assumptions. | Supported by published theoretical calculations. |
| A positive gravitational-entanglement result is sufficient to prove quantum gravity. | Not established; the inference is contested and requires model comparison. |
| Unimodular gravity provides a classical reformulation in which the cosmological constant appears as an integration constant. | Established classical result; does not determine its observed value. |
| The proposed BHC source is consistent for general Bohmian configurations. | Unproven. |
| ALG provides a complete nonperturbative quantum theory of gravity. | Not established. |
| A common mathematical foundation can unify matter, spacetime and dynamics in all regimes. | Research hypothesis. |
| Type III modular structure resolves the actualization problem. | Speculative. |

This distinction is especially important.

We have not discovered a new complete theory of gravity.

We have developed a more disciplined way to investigate the problem, identified important mathematical connections, and exposed several assumptions that competing theories must confront.

The proposed BHC and ALG cores are still research specifications, not completed mathematical theories.

# 10. What should happen next?

Our most productive next steps are not to invent additional assumptions or expand the architecture indefinitely.

We should start producing independently verifiable mathematical results.

**First: solve the matter-spacetime consistency problem.**

For BHC, derive the proposed stress-energy source from its actual configuration and guidance dynamics.

Determine whether it satisfies energy-momentum conservation and under which conditions.

This is an architectural blocker. Without a consistent source, the proposed classical-gravity construction cannot proceed.

**Second: investigate gravitational entanglement identifiability.**

Reconstruct the competing predictions independently.

Determine whether mass, geometry, time and material composition can distinguish quantum gravity from the strongest classical alternatives.

Do not assume that observing entanglement settles the question.

**Third: establish the clock mathematics.**

Determine whether different internal clocks produce equivalent physical descriptions and identify precisely where that equivalence fails.

**Fourth: test the black-hole information problem.**

Determine whether either approach can reproduce the relevant entropy and information results without importing assumptions that contradict its own foundations.

**Fifth: investigate the cosmological constant.**

Test unimodular gravity as a specific mathematical proposal rather than assuming it solves vacuum energy.

Record exactly what it explains and what it leaves undetermined.

Each stage must produce a concrete derivation, a solvable example, an independently checkable result and a record of unresolved mathematical obligations.

# 11. The bigger picture

The most significant insight from our investigation is that the problem of unifying physics may be as much about the relationships between mathematical structures as it is about finding the correct equations.

Matter, spacetime and dynamics each have powerful mathematical descriptions.

But a complete physical theory must explain how they interact without losing the information that makes each description meaningful.

This leads to a broader possibility.

Perhaps the next major development in fundamental physics will not come from replacing every existing theory with one entirely new mathematical object.

Perhaps it will come from discovering the precise mathematical connections that allow existing structures to work together, while revealing where new structures are genuinely required.

That is the direction we are investigating.

The test is straightforward:

Can we start with clearly defined assumptions, translate between the mathematical descriptions, derive known physical results, make new falsifiable predictions and survive adversarial mathematical review?

Until we can do that, we should not claim to have a theory of everything.

But we will have something valuable: a systematic way to discover what a successful theory of everything would actually need to explain.

**The objective is not to make mathematics describe what we want reality to be. It is to make our mathematics accountable to what reality allows.**
