# Why Does Time Only Go Forward? The Honest Answer Is Weirder Than You Think

*A plain-language tour of what physics actually knows about time — and where a popular-science essay called "Dual Kernel Theory" gets it right, gets it wrong, and quietly leaves out the hardest part*

---

## The feeling nobody can explain

You remember yesterday. You don't remember tomorrow. Coffee cools; it doesn't spontaneously reheat. Eggs break; they don't reassemble. Nothing in your daily life suggests that time might run backwards.

And yet, if you look at the equations physicists actually use, none of them has an arrow in it.

That is the scandal at the heart of modern physics. Our deepest theories — the ones that predict the color of a flame, the orbit of Mercury, the mass of a proton — are almost all **time-symmetric**. They work exactly as well running forwards as backwards. If you filmed a collision between two billiard balls and played it in reverse, the reversed film would obey the same laws. There is no "Play forward only" button in the rulebook.

So why does the universe have a forward direction at all?

This is not a philosophical footnote. It is one of the deepest open questions in physics, and a recent essay by Bill Giannakopoulos — a popular-science piece proposing something called "Dual Kernel Theory" — has taken a serious swing at it. The essay is well-written, honest about its limits, and gets several things genuinely right. But it also has one big hole, and one piece of jargon pretending to be a theory. This article walks through what it says, what it gets right, and what the honest answer actually is.

---

## Part 1: Three clocks that don't agree

Here is the first thing worth understanding, because almost every popular account gets it wrong. **The "problem of time" is not one problem. It is three.**

**Clock one: the quantum clock.** The equation that governs the quantum world — Schrödinger's equation — describes a system evolving smoothly and reversibly. Given the state now, you can run the equation backwards and recover the state a moment ago. No information is lost. No arrow is built in.

**Clock two: the thermodynamic clock.** Heat flows from hot to cold. Entropy — a measure of how many ways a system can be arranged — increases. This is the Second Law of Thermodynamics, and it has been confirmed to the point of absurdity: no one has ever caught a macroscopic system spontaneously decreasing in entropy. This clock has a strong, definite arrow.

**Clock three: the relativistic clock.** In Einstein's picture, time is not a flowing river but a coordinate in a four-dimensional block. There is no "now" that sweeps through the universe; there are just events, located in a geometry. This clock has no arrow at all — spacetime is a static sculpture.

Each of these clocks is extraordinarily successful in its own domain. But they do not fit together. The quantum clock says "reversible." The thermodynamic clock says "irreversible." The relativistic clock says "there is no flow at all." You cannot get a coherent picture just by stacking them.

The first thing a good theory of time must do, then, is **not confuse these three problems with each other.** The Dual Kernel essay gets this right. Many popular accounts do not. That is already a point in its favor.

## Part 2: The two-clock trick — how time can exist without a master clock

Now for the most important technical idea in the essay, and one of the most beautiful in modern physics. It comes from two physicists, Don Page and William Wootters, in 1983.

Here is the puzzle they were trying to solve. In some approaches to quantum gravity, the universe has a single quantum state that does not evolve. The whole thing is frozen. The equation — called the Wheeler–DeWitt equation, if you want the name — says the universe's state is stationary. Nothing happens.

And yet things obviously happen. So how?

**Here is the analogy that makes it click.** Imagine a book of photographs, but not a movie — a single bound volume, each page showing a room from a slightly different angle, taken at a slightly different moment. The book as a whole is a static object. It does not change. It just sits on the shelf.

But now imagine you are *inside* one of the photographs. You can only see your own page. You cannot see the book. And you have a little clock with you — an alarm clock, say, that shows the time of the photograph you're in.

If the book was assembled carefully, the pages are *correlated*. When the alarm clock shows 3:00, the coffee cup is on the table. When it shows 3:05, the cup is at your lips. When it shows 3:10, it's half empty. Each page on its own is frozen. But the *sequence of correlations* between the clock and the cup looks exactly like motion.

That is the Page–Wootters trick. **Time is not a global thing that flows. Time is a relationship between two parts of a frozen whole.** When one part functions as a clock, the rest of the universe appears to evolve relative to it.

This is not a metaphor. It is a precise mathematical construction. And it has been built explicitly in physical models — the so-called "clock-system" models that appear throughout quantum gravity. The frozen universe is real, and so is the internal appearance of time.

**But here is the crucial point, and the one almost every popular account gets wrong:** Page–Wootters explains how you get **ordering** — how you get "before" and "after" without a master clock. It does not explain why the "after" has *more entropy* than the "before."

It tells you the film reel is a sequence of frames. It does not tell you why the film is playing in one direction rather than the other.

That gap — between ordering and direction — is the real arrow-of-time problem. And this is exactly where the Dual Kernel essay tries to make its contribution.

## Part 3: Ink in water — the mechanism everyone agrees on

The essay's answer to the arrow is built on a well-established process that most readers have seen but not named: **decoherence**.

Imagine dropping a drop of ink into a glass of water. At first, the ink is a compact, ordered blob. Then it spreads. The dye molecules disperse through the water. After a few minutes, the water is uniformly tinted, and the original drop is gone.

Here is the crucial fact, which surprises people: **the ink is not destroyed.** The information about where each dye molecule started is still present in the water — in principle, all of it is recoverable. If you knew the exact position and momentum of every water molecule and every dye molecule, you could run the process backwards and reconstruct the drop. The laws of physics permit it.

But you cannot do it in practice, because you would need to track an astronomically large number of microscopic variables, and any measurement error would be amplified faster than you could act. The information isn't gone; it's **smeared across degrees of freedom you cannot reach.**

This is exactly what decoherence does to a quantum system. A quantum state's delicate phase relationships get entangled with the environment, and once the environment is large enough, the phase information becomes inaccessible to any local observer. From the observer's point of view, the system looks classical — the quantum interference is gone.

Decoherence is real. It is measured. It is standard physics, accepted by everyone from Zurek to the quantum-gravity community. When your coffee cup looks classical instead of quantum, it is because it has entangled with roughly \(10^{25}\) air molecules, and you cannot un-entangle it.

**This is the machinery the Dual Kernel essay is gesturing toward.** And the essay tells this story well. When it says "correlations disperse into the environment and become inaccessible," it is describing decoherence — accurately, and in language that non-physicists can follow.

So what's missing?

## Part 4: The hole — why the ink started as a drop

Here is the problem. The mechanism of decoherence explains how a *low-entropy state* disperses into a *high-entropy state*. But it works just as well in reverse — under the exact same laws, a high-entropy state disperses back into a low-entropy state. The equations do not prefer one direction.

So why does the universe always show the forward version?

**Because the ink started as a drop.**

If the initial state of the universe had been a uniform, high-entropy, featureless soup, there would be no arrow. Entropy would have nowhere to increase to; the system would already be at maximum dispersal. The reason we see time flowing, eggs breaking, and coffee cooling is that the universe *began* in an extraordinarily special, low-entropy configuration.

This is called the **Past Hypothesis.** It was articulated by Boltzmann, sharpened by Feynman and Penrose, and given its modern precise form by the philosopher David Albert. The idea is simple to state and devastating in its implications:

> The arrow of time exists because the universe started in a very special state. If it had started in a typical state, there would be no arrow.

Nobody knows *why* the universe started in such a special state. This is one of the deepest unsolved problems in all of physics. It is sometimes called the "arrow of time problem," but the honest name is the "low-entropy initial condition problem."

And here is the crucial point: **the Dual Kernel essay never mentions it.**

The essay says the arrow is "the migration of correlations from K₁ to K₀." But correlations migrate in both directions. The reason we see one direction rather than the other has nothing to do with the K₁/K₀ distinction. It has everything to do with the fact that the initial condition was low-entropy.

The essay relocates the puzzle. It does not solve it. The Past Hypothesis is the missing piece, and its absence is the largest gap in the argument.

## Part 5: The clock's price — a genuinely beautiful result

The essay's strongest section is about clocks. And the result it describes is real, elegant, and worth understanding in full.

Here is the question. A clock is a physical device. It ticks. What does it cost?

Naively, a clock is just a periodic mechanism — a pendulum, a quartz crystal, a caesium atom oscillating between two energy levels. It seems like it should be able to tick without any thermodynamic price, just by oscillating back and forth.

But here is the subtlety. A clock doesn't just oscillate. It **records** its oscillations. Each tick must be distinguishable from the next — that's what "tick" means. And to make a tick distinguishable, the clock must interact with its environment in a way that leaves a record.

Leaving a record has a thermodynamic cost. This is **Landauer's principle**, one of the most beautiful results in modern physics: erasing a single bit of information costs a minimum amount of energy, which is dissipated as heat. (Rolf Landauer discovered this in 1961; it was experimentally confirmed by Bérut and collaborators in 2012.)

Put these together, and you get a remarkable theorem, proved by Paul Skrzypczyk, Ralph Silva, and Nicolas Brunner in 2011:

> **A clock that ticks with precision \(\epsilon\) must dissipate at least a certain amount of heat per tick to its environment.**

The more accurate the clock, the more entropy it must produce. There is no free tick.

**Here is the intuition.** Imagine trying to keep time with a sundial. The sundial requires no energy — it just sits there. But it also doesn't work at night, doesn't work in clouds, doesn't work indoors. It's cheap but unreliable. Now imagine a mechanical watch. It works anywhere, anytime, in the dark, in the rain — but its gears heat up slightly as it runs. You paid for the accuracy with entropy.

This is not a philosophical curiosity. It is a real, quantitative, experimentally grounded result. And the essay describes it accurately.

**The essay's overreach**, however, comes when it generalizes. The precise theorem is about specific models of quantum clocks in thermal baths. The essay suggests it shows that "observable temporal order requires irreversible record creation" universally. That is a plausible conjecture. It is not a theorem. The essay does not distinguish, and it should.

## Part 6: The K₁/K₀ problem — why "regimes" aren't structures

Here is where the essay commits the sin the reality-checks call "coining private structure faster than theorems accrue behind the names."

The essay proposes two "regimes" called K₁ and K₀:

- **K₁** is the "coherent" regime, where correlations stay accessible and dynamics look reversible.
- **K₀** is the "dispersed" regime, where correlations spread into environments and become computationally inaccessible.

These are evocative names. They sound like a new theory. But **they are not a mathematical structure.**

Here is what I mean. In physics, when we say a structure is real, we can point to something specific: a set of states, an operation that transforms them, a set of observables, a measure of probability, a complexity class. K₁ and K₀ are none of these. They are **labels for two ends of a well-known spectrum** — the spectrum between "decoherent" and "coherent" — dressed up as if they were new objects.

To see the problem, translate the essay's claim into plain terms:

> "Time flows because correlations migrate from K₁ to K₀."

Substitute the definitions:

> "Time flows because correlations migrate from 'the accessible regime' to 'the inaccessible regime.'"

Now ask: what mechanism drives the migration? What is the operation? What structure holds the correlations at each end? The essay doesn't say. It cannot say, because K₁ and K₀ were defined by *pointing* at two familiar phenomena (coherence and decoherence) rather than by *constructing* a mathematical object.

**This is not a knock on the essay's honesty.** The essay itself says it is a "conceptual proposal rather than a completed theory." That is the right epistemic stance. But it also means the reader should not walk away believing they have learned a new structure. They have learned a new vocabulary for an old structure.

**Here is what K₁/K₀ actually names, translated into the existing physics vocabulary:**

- **Decoherence** — the loss of phase coherence under environmental coupling. This is standard open-quantum-systems theory (Zurek, Joos, Zeh, Schlosshauer).
- **Entanglement growth** — the growth of quantum correlations between a system and its environment. Measured by entanglement entropy; bounded by the smaller subsystem's size.
- **Computational hardness** — the exponential difficulty of decoding information once it has scrambled. Proven by Harlow and Hayden (2013) for black holes; connected to the "chaos bound" of Maldacena, Shenker, and Stanford (2016).

All three of these are well-developed. The Dual Kernel essay's K₁ and K₀ are, roughly, "the regime where these processes haven't happened yet" and "the regime where they have." That is a real distinction, but it's not new — and calling it a "theory" overstates what it does.

## Part 7: What a real solution would look like

This is the most important section for a lay reader. What would count as actually *solving* the arrow of time?

Here is the honest answer, stripped of jargon. A complete theory of the arrow would need to provide all four of the following:

**1. A precise definition of entropy.** Entropy is only defined once you choose a level of coarse-graining — a way of grouping microstates into macrostates. Different coarse-grainings give different entropies. The theory must say which coarse-graining is physical.

**2. A demonstration that entropy increases.** This follows from reversible laws plus coarse-graining, but the derivation must be carried out carefully — it involves the fact that almost all microstates compatible with a given macrostate will evolve into states compatible with a higher-entropy macrostate, under a generic Hamiltonian.

**3. A low-entropy initial condition.** This is the Past Hypothesis. Without it, the previous step proves nothing about the actual universe — it only proves that entropy *would* increase *if* the universe started in a special state. Something must make it special.

**4. A derivation of the initial condition.** Ideally from a deeper principle. This is the part we do not have. Nobody knows why the early universe had such extraordinarily low entropy — the famous "gravitational clumping" that made the Big Bang so special.

The Dual Kernel essay addresses point 2 in some detail (its "migration" story is essentially a restatement of point 2) and gestures vaguely at point 1 (the "computationally inaccessible" language). It does not address point 3 at all. And nobody, in any essay, has point 4.

**A complete solution to the arrow would provide all four.** The best current frameworks provide 1, 2, and 3, and honestly flag 4 as unsolved. The Dual Kernel essay provides roughly 1 and 2, and silently omits 3.

## Part 8: The relativity gap

There is one more omission worth flagging, because it affects how much the essay's story can claim to accomplish.

The essay opens by naming three tensions: quantum reversibility, thermodynamic irreversibility, and the relativistic block universe. Then it addresses the first two and lets the third drop.

But relativity matters for the arrow, and here is why.

The Second Law says entropy increases "forward in time." But which "forward"? In a relativistic universe, there is no universal "now" — different observers slice spacetime into "past" and "future" differently. So whose entropy increases?

The honest answer, worked out in the cosmological literature (by Penrose, by Carroll and Chen, by Earman), is that the arrow is only well-defined in a **cosmological** setting — that is, relative to the expansion of the universe and a particular family of observers (the "comoving" observers who see the cosmic microwave background as isotropic). The arrow is not a fact about the local laws of physics. It is a fact about **the cosmological boundary condition**, evaluated along a specific slicing.

This means the arrow is *both* a local statistical fact (decoherence and entropy production) *and* a global cosmological fact (the low-entropy initial condition and the well-defined slicing on which it's evaluated). You cannot fully explain the arrow without both.

The Dual Kernel essay handles the local side. It says nothing about the cosmological side. That's fine for a popular essay — but it means the framework is, at most, half the story.

## Part 9: What the essay gets right, and why it's still worth reading

Let me be clear about the essay's virtues, because they are real.

**Virtue 1: It separates the three problems correctly.** Most popular physics writing conflates the frozen-universe problem, the emergence problem, and the arrow problem. The essay keeps them distinct. This is more disciplined than most professional philosophy of physics writing.

**Virtue 2: It states Page–Wootters correctly.** Time as correlations, not as a background parameter, is subtle — and easy to state incorrectly. The essay states it correctly. It even says, explicitly, that Page–Wootters gives ordering but not direction.

**Virtue 3: It describes the clock-thermodynamics result accurately.** This is a genuinely non-obvious theorem — accurate clocks must dissipate heat — and the essay conveys it without oversimplifying.

**Virtue 4: Its open-questions section is honest.** Most popular-science essays on time end with a triumphant claim. This one ends with a list of open problems. That is the right epistemic posture.

**Virtue 5: It is accessible.** The essay does not require a physics degree. It uses everyday language, it explains the setup, and it does not resort to hand-waving dressed as authority. For a reader who wants to understand the shape of the problem without the technical machinery, the essay is a good place to start.

**The honest counterpoint:** The essay's central proposal — K₁/K₀ — is more evocative than it is substantive. It does not introduce a new mathematical structure. It does not derive the arrow. It does not mention the Past Hypothesis. And its consciousness section, at the end, is pure speculation without a mechanism.

The essay is, in short, a very good *introduction* to the arrow-of-time problem, and a somewhat overambitious attempt at a *solution*.

---

## Part 10: What you should actually take away

If you have read this far and want the short version, here it is.

**Time's arrow is not an explanatory mystery in the way it sounds.** The mechanism — decoherence, entanglement, entropy production — is well understood. We know *how* correlations disperse. We know *why* that dispersal looks irreversible from the inside of any subsystem. We even know how much energy must be paid to keep time accurately.

**The genuine mystery is one question: why did the universe start in such a low-entropy state?**

This is not a question the Dual Kernel essay answers, and it's not a question anyone currently answers. It is the arrow-of-time problem in its deepest form. And it is a *cosmological* problem, not a *quantum statistical mechanics* problem. Its solution, if it comes, will likely come from quantum gravity — from a theory that explains why the initial state of the universe was special.

**Every popular account that does not mention the Past Hypothesis is incomplete.** Every popular account that does mention it is honest. The Dual Kernel essay belongs to the first group, despite being unusually well-written within that group.

**Time, at bottom, is not a substance that flows.** It is a *relationship* between two parts of the universe, made to look directional by the fact that one part — the universe at the beginning — was in an extraordinarily unlikely state. The clock you're holding, the coffee cooling on the table, the memories in your head — all of them are participants in a vast dispersal of correlations, from a compact beginning to a spread-out future. The dispersal is the arrow. The beginning is the mystery.

And the beginning is what nobody has explained.
